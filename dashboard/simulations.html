Every matchup, played 10,000 times.A play-by-play simulation of each game on the slate — not a rating formula. Every snap is called the way that offensive coordinator calls it (pass rate over expected, pace, red-zone habits, fourth-down nerve), thrown to the men on this week's depth chart by their real target and carry shares, against what that defensive coordinator's coverage family and pressure rate actually concede to each slot. Out of it comes the most likely final score, the full box score, win and cover probabilities, and the whole distribution. Run any game yourself with the same engine, right here in the browser.

How it works — the league's play-calling, yardage, completion, turnover, kicking and clock tables come from every nflverse play since 2024 (119,454 snaps) · each team's offense and defense is its coordinator's profile (a new coordinator carries his last unit's habits until this season's games outweigh them) · players are the current depth chart with injuries applied — a missing starter's targets flow to the men still on the chart · one league-level calibration keeps an average matchup at the league's actual dropback share, yards per play and touchdowns · the backtest below re-runs every week already played with only the data that existed then, and grades it against the result and the closing line.

method & limits

One snap. Down, distance, field zone, score state and clock pick the league dropback probability from 1045 charted situations; the offensive coordinator's pass rate over expected shifts it (his red-zone pass rate takes over inside the 20). A dropback is a sack, a scramble or a throw at the matchup's rates. A throw picks its target by his share of the team's targets, scaled by what the defense's coverage family concedes to that slot; it is completed at the league rate for the spot, shifted by the quarterback, the defense and the receiver's own catch rate, or intercepted at the matchup's rate. Yards come from the league's yardage quantiles for that zone and down, scaled by offensive EPA against defensive EPA allowed and the player's own yards per target or carry, with explosive plays drawn at the matchup's explosive rate. Inside the 20 the touchdown is drawn by the spot (league P(TD) from 1–3, 4–6, 7–10, 11–15, 16–20 yards out) times the two teams' red-zone conversion.

Everything else. Fourth downs follow the league go-rate for the distance and zone times the coach's measured aggressiveness; field goals make at the league rate by distance; punts, kickoff starts, return touchdowns and fumbles at league rates; the clock runs at the league's seconds-between-snaps by situation times the offense's pace, with hurry-up when trailing late; halftime, two-point tries when the score calls for them, kneel-downs with a lead inside the last minute, overtime with a possession for each side. Home field is the league's measured edge split across both offenses.

Coaches. Each unit is a blend of this season under its coordinator (weight games/(games+3)) and the coordinator's own unit last season — the team he came from if he is new, the same team if not. So a first-year coordinator starts as his old offense and becomes this one as the games pile up.

The quarterback. The passing game is attached to the man taking the snaps: when the named starter is not the passer the unit has mostly used, the offense's passing EPA moves by the gap between the two (each quarterback's EPA per dropback over 2025–26, shrunk toward replacement level), so a backup under center is priced as a backup, not as the man he replaced. Defensive starters ruled out loosen the defense: a missing first-string defensive back raises passing EPA allowed, a missing front-seven starter raises rushing EPA allowed.

Rest. A team on a short week (≤5 days) is shaded 1.5% down on yards, a team off a bye (≥10 days) 1.5% up — a small effect the backtest accepted.

How changes get in. Every candidate signal is run through the backtest (weeks 4 onward, parameters as of each week) and kept only if the margin and total errors improve; nothing is ever fitted to the market. The log below is the record.

Calibration. Three league-level scalars (dropback share, yards per play, red-zone touchdowns) are fitted so that an average team against an average team reproduces the league's actual per-game numbers over the last two seasons. They are printed under every game's "what it used". Nothing is tuned to the market.

Not modelled. Penalties, weather, timeouts, in-game injuries, specific kickers and returners, individual defenders (the defense is its coordinator's unit), and anything after the depth chart snapshot the page names. Exact-score probabilities are thin by nature — the most common final is about 1% of simulations — so the distribution is the forecast, the modal score is its headline.
